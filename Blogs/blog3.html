What should you actually spend on digital marketing?

There is no magic number that every business should spend on marketing. The better question is what your budget is supposed to achieve — and whether you're spending it in the right places.

15 NOV 2026 V DO MARKETING 6 MIN READ

One of the first questions businesses ask when they start thinking seriously about digital marketing is simple: "How much should we spend?"

The problem is that there is no single answer. A local business trying to generate enquiries does not have the same needs as an e-commerce brand trying to scale online. A new business does not have the same priorities as an established brand.

Yet marketing budgets are often treated like a fixed number. Businesses either spend as little as possible, spend too much too quickly, or copy what another company is doing without understanding why.

Your marketing budget should have a job.

There isn't a magic marketing number

You may have heard advice about spending a certain percentage of revenue on marketing.

That can be useful as a starting point, but it should never become the entire strategy.

Your marketing budget should make sense for your current situation, your goals, your audience and the channels you actually need.

Instead of asking, "What percentage should we spend?" start with: "What are we trying to achieve?"

Start with the goal, not the budget

Your marketing goal changes what you should spend money on.

If the goal is to improve brand awareness, your focus may be on creative content, video, social media and consistent brand communication.

If the goal is generating enquiries, you may need a stronger website, landing pages, paid advertising, content and a clear conversion path.

If the goal is long-term organic growth, SEO and useful content may deserve more attention.

And if you're trying to scale an e-commerce business, paid acquisition, creative testing, conversion optimisation and retention may become much more important.

The point is simple: the budget should follow the objective.

Where does your marketing budget actually go?

Marketing spend is not just the money you put into Meta Ads or Google Ads.

There are several parts of the marketing system that need to work together.

  • Content and creative: graphics, videos, photography, copy and campaign assets.
  • Social media: planning, publishing, community management and ongoing brand communication.
  • Paid advertising: Google Ads, Meta Ads and other paid acquisition channels.
  • SEO: improving your visibility in search and building useful long-term organic traffic.
  • Website and landing pages: making sure people have somewhere useful to go after discovering your business.
  • Influencer and affiliate activity: using relevant partners to introduce your brand to new audiences.

If one part is weak, the performance of the others can suffer.

There is little point in sending paid traffic to a website that does not explain the offer clearly.

There is also little point in creating beautiful social content if nobody knows what your business actually does.

Cheap marketing can become expensive marketing

Trying to save money is understandable. But the cheapest option is not always the most efficient one.

For example, a business might spend very little on creative, create a few rushed ads and launch a campaign. When the campaign does not perform, the conclusion becomes: "Ads don't work."

But the real problem may have been the creative, the offer, the landing page, the targeting or the message.

Marketing works as a system. Looking at one number without understanding the system can lead to the wrong conclusion.

Don't put your entire budget into ads

Paid advertising can generate results quickly, which makes it attractive to businesses.

But ads are not a replacement for everything else.

You still need a reason for people to care. You still need strong creative. You still need a clear offer. And you still need somewhere to send people.

Think of paid media as one part of the engine, not the entire engine.

A balanced digital marketing approach can combine paid acquisition with content, SEO, social media, creative and conversion-focused website experiences.

A BETTER QUESTION

Instead of asking, "Did this campaign work immediately?" ask, "What did this campaign teach us, and what should we improve next?"

Testing is part of the budget

One mistake businesses make is expecting every marketing dollar to produce an immediate result.

Good marketing often involves testing.

You may need to test different messages, audiences, creative directions, landing pages, offers or channels before you know what consistently works.

That does not mean wasting money. It means giving your strategy enough room to learn.

Know what you're actually measuring

Not every marketing activity should be judged by the same metric.

A brand awareness campaign may be focused on reach, attention and engagement.

A lead generation campaign may be focused on enquiries, qualified leads and cost per lead.

An e-commerce campaign may be focused on purchases, revenue and return on ad spend.

SEO may take longer to show meaningful results than a paid campaign.

If you measure everything only by immediate sales, you can end up cutting activities that are actually helping build the business over time.

When should you increase your budget?

Increasing your marketing budget should not simply happen because you have more money available.

It should happen when you have enough evidence that the current approach is working and that additional investment has a reasonable chance of creating more opportunity.

For example, if a campaign is producing quality enquiries, your creative is working, your website is converting and your sales process can handle more leads, increasing the budget may make sense.

But if the fundamentals are not working, spending more can simply make the problem more expensive.

A better way to think about your marketing budget

Instead of choosing one large number and trying to spend it, think about your marketing budget in layers.

  • Build: create the brand, website, creative assets and messaging your business needs.
  • Test: experiment with channels, audiences, offers and creative.
  • Measure: understand what is producing attention, enquiries, customers or other meaningful outcomes.
  • Scale: put more resources behind the areas that are showing genuine potential.

This approach gives your budget a job.

You are no longer simply "spending money on marketing." You are investing in a process that should become clearer and more effective over time.

BEFORE YOU SPEND MORE

Make sure you know what you're trying to achieve, who you're trying to reach, what you're measuring and where the biggest opportunity actually is.

Before you increase your marketing spend, ask yourself this

Look at your current marketing approach and ask:

  • What is the main goal of our marketing right now?
  • Who are we actually trying to reach?
  • Is our message clear enough?
  • Does our creative represent the quality of our business?
  • Is our website ready to convert the attention we generate?
  • Are we measuring the right outcome?
  • Have we tested enough before deciding something does not work?
  • Can our business handle the extra demand if marketing works?

Your budget should support your strategy

The right marketing budget is not necessarily the biggest one.

It is the one that gives your business enough room to create, test, learn and improve without spending blindly.

Sometimes that means starting smaller and learning first. Sometimes it means investing more because the foundations are already working.

What matters is understanding why you're spending, where the money is going and what you expect it to achieve.

Your marketing budget doesn't need to be huge. It needs to have a reason behind it.
